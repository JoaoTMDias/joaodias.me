A few days ago, I read [an NPR report about Cody and Kaleigh Brendle](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/20/nx-s1-5891791/disability-rights-community-living-integration-trump), two siblings I have never met, but whose story stayed with me.

Both are blind, were born in the United States and grew up under the **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)**, passed in 1990.

Cody, the older of the two, is 27 and lives with his parents. He takes part in a community programme that supports him with activities such as shopping, attending dance classes and receiving vocational training. Kaleigh, 23, was preparing to travel across the country to study law when NPR followed their story.

The report describes them as part of the “ADA generation”: people who grew up in a country where the unnecessary segregation of people with disabilities came to be treated, at least legally, as discrimination.

That is what makes their story relevant now.

That principle is being challenged.

I am not saying this because I have a romanticised view of the United States. I do not, particularly after the past decade. The American history of civil rights is full of contradictions, exclusion and violence, and many of those problems remain unresolved.

But there are American achievements in disability rights that have influenced far more than the United States itself. The ADA was one of them. Another was the Supreme Court's 1999 decision in *Olmstead v. L.C.*

![Blind person with a white cane leaving a building in an outdoor urban space.](/artigo-europa-\(1\).webp "Independent living starts with being able to enter, leave and move around your community independently. Source: Unsplash")

## The right to be part of the community

The idea behind *Olmstead* is simple: a person with a disability should not be forced to live in an institution simply because the support they need is not available in their community.

For decades, that was the interpretation of [Title II of the ADA and the](https://www.ada.gov/resources/olmstead-mandate-statement/) *[Olmstead](https://www.ada.gov/resources/olmstead-mandate-statement/)* decision followed by the US Department of Justice. Public services should be provided in the most integrated setting possible. The Supreme Court held that unjustified segregation constitutes a form of discrimination.

In practice, we are talking about the difference between saying:

> “We have somewhere where we can take care of you.”

and saying:

> “We will create the conditions you need to live your life in your neighbourhood, with your family, with your friends.”

The difference is bigger than it may seem.

The second sentence is about choosing where to live and with whom, studying, working, leaving the house, going shopping, having relationships, making plans. Things most of us simply call living.

![Wheelchair user talking to another person in a social setting, surrounded by several other people.](/artigo-europa-\(3\).webp "Living in the community also means this: taking part in social life, meeting people and sharing the same spaces as everyone else. Source: Unsplash")

In June 2026, the Office of Legal Counsel at the US Department of Justice [published a memorandum](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/application-rehabilitation-act-and-americans-disabilities-act-state) challenging this interpretation. Under this new legal reading, neither the *Rehabilitation Act* nor the ADA imposes a binding obligation on states to provide integration.

The memorandum does not repeal the law or erase *Olmstead*. But it changes the federal government's position on what those obligations mean.

That has consequences.

If the obligation to integrate is weakened at the same time as community services lose funding, more people may end up in institutions without having chosen to live there. Not because that is the best solution, but because a viable alternative no longer exists.

Medicaid is central to this problem.

The programme funds a large share of home and community-based services in the US. According to the [Congressional Budget Office's projections](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62105) for 2026, the planned changes are expected to reduce public spending by **$1.2 trillion between 2026 and 2035**, while leaving more than 13 million people without that protection.

It was this combination that made me uneasy: less money for support in the community, while the federal government steps back from the idea that integration is an obligation.

## What about us?

My reaction to the report was immediate: **are we doing the same thing?**

I went looking.

The answer is not a simple “no”.

Europe still institutionalises too many people. European Commission data indicates that [more than one million people with disabilities under the age of 65 live in institutions in the European Union](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/together-independent-living_pt). If we include people over 65, that figure rises to around three million.

In May 2026, the Commission itself was still acknowledging that [1.4 million people live away from the community](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-proposes-new-measures-fight-poverty-and-improve-lives-persons-disabilities-2026-05-06_en).

You do not need to open a report to see the problem either. Just look at pavements that a wheelchair cannot navigate, public transport that is impossible to board, the lack of personal assistants, or families that end up carrying almost all the responsibility for support that should not depend on them alone.

So no, Europe has not solved this.

But there is an important difference in direction.

Europe's current political and legal framework starts from the principle that a person with a disability should be able to live in the community and receive the support they need there.

## Independent living is not charity

The European Union and its Member States are bound by the **United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities**.

[Article 19 of the Convention](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-19-living-independently-and-being-included-in-the-community.html) recognises the right to live in the community and to have access to the support needed to do so.

It is not a favour. It is a right.

The [European Strategy 2021-2030](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/together-rights_pt) points in the same direction. The [guidance published by the Commission in 2024](https://accessible-eu-centre.ec.europa.eu/content-corner/news/commission-adopts-guidance-independent-living-persons-disabilities-2024-11-29_en) makes clear that European public funding should not be used to perpetuate institutions, but to develop alternatives in the community.

That does not mean the system works well.

It means the principle has been established.

And that principle did not appear because governments suddenly decided to be generous. It was won by people with disabilities who organised, protested and demanded the right to decide where and how they wanted to live.

## And in Portugal?

![Person using a powered wheelchair entering a train via an access ramp.](/artigo-europa-\(2\).webp "Accessibility depends on practical solutions that allow people to use public transport on equal terms. Photo: Lisboa Para Pessoas.")

Portugal also has important measures in place.

The **Independent Living Support Model (MAVI)**, created by [Decree-Law No. 129/2017](https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/detalhe/decreto-lei/129-2017-108265124), and the regulation of **Independent Living Support Centres (CAVI)** through [Ministerial Order No. 415/2023](https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/detalhe/portaria/415-2023-225397119) represent an important change in principle: providing support so that people can make decisions about their own lives, instead of organising their lives for them.

The same shift appears in other areas, from inclusive education ([Decree-Law No. 54/2018](https://www.parlamento.pt/Legislacao/Paginas/Educacao_Geral_Educacao-Inclusiva.aspx)) to digital accessibility ([Decree-Law No. 83/2018](https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/detalhe/decreto-lei/83-2018-116734769)) and the [European Accessibility Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/PT/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32019L0882).

I work on the web, so this difference between a rule existing and a rule actually working is familiar to me.

We can have a specification, a standard or a mandatory requirement. That does not mean the implementation is any good.

Something similar happens with rights.

A law in the Diário da República does not install a ramp in a building, hire a personal assistant or make a bus accessible.

The [2025 Report on People with Disabilities in Portugal](https://oddh.iscsp.ulisboa.pt/publicacoes/publicacoes-oddh-pt/relatorio-pessoas-com-deficiencia-em-portugal-indicadores-de-direitos-humanos-2025/) indicates that Portugal spends 1.49% of GDP on disability-related social protection, below the European average of 1.88%.

The [September 2026 monitoring report](https://oddh.iscsp.ulisboa.pt/noticias-e-eventos/noticias-pt/divulgacao-relatorio-sobre-a-implementacao-da-convencao-sobre-os-direitos-das-pessoas-com-deficiencia-em-portugal-e-de-atividade-do-mecanismo-nacional-de-monitorizacao-da-implementacao-da-convencao/) once again points to problems with the implementation of the Convention in Portugal.

We have legislation, principles and tools.

The difficult part is still making them work.

## This is the right path, but we're not even halfway there

That is what I ended up taking away from Cody and Kaleigh's story.

Rights do not disappear only when someone repeals a law.

They can also be hollowed out gradually. An interpretation changes. Funding is cut. An obligation stops being enforced. An alternative disappears.

In the end, something that used to be a choice no longer is.

Europe is nowhere near being able to celebrate. We still institutionalise people. We still fail on accessibility. We still rely too heavily on families. We still do not adequately fund the support that makes independent living possible.

But there is a difference between failing to deliver on a right and starting to question whether that right should be an obligation of the state at all.

I would rather live in a country where we have to fight to make the government enforce the law properly than in one where the state itself starts questioning people's right to live in freedom.
